The short version

Most people building AI agents have never made software touch the physical world. For two years that was the only kind of software I wrote, and it is still what I design around: a constraint you cannot argue with, a deadline that is a public event, and a machine that either works in front of people or does not.

The work on this page follows one pattern, and it is worth spelling out because it is the thing a client is actually buying. First, find the constraint that binds. It is almost never the one in the brief. On the humanoid it was not face recognition, it was 1 GB of RAM. On the attendance system it was not the algorithm, it was a library that would not install. On the servo arm it was not motion, it was the wireless ceiling of the board. Second, remove that constraint directly, even when the direct route is slow and unglamorous: a four-hour DLIB compile on the Pi itself, a UART protocol written from nothing because no library did what the joints needed, a whole codebase moved from an Arduino Mega to an ESP32 mid-build rather than waiting for a better board. Third, keep the rest of the system boring, so that the only new risk is the one you chose.

That order matters more than any single skill. Reading the field comes before writing code, measuring the hardware comes before choosing a model, and a working thing on a bench comes before a slide about it. It is also why I am honest about speed: a field next to technology is productive in days, a field far from it costs reading time first.

Now I run that habit as a practice. A client brings a field I may not know, and seven days later there is something on a bench that runs. The arm beside this panel is a small version of the same idea: change its link lengths in the controls and the inverse kinematics, the reach limits and the working volume are all recomputed from four numbers, live, the way a real rig is re-specified when the brief changes.

Research practice

How a sprint runs, and what it costs when the field is far from tech.

Seven days Fixed

The sprint

A sprint is seven days, fixed, and the deliverable is the thing that runs. Fixing the length rather than the scope is deliberate: it forces the first day to decide what is worth answering, instead of letting the question grow until the week is gone.

How the week is spent

  1. Day 1, read and scope. Learn the vocabulary of the field well enough to ask the client the right questions. Name the one constraint that binds, whether it is memory, latency, power, connectivity or a regulation, and write down the single question the prototype has to answer.
  2. Days 2 to 4, build on real hardware. Not a simulation and not a notebook. The cheapest board that could plausibly do the job comes first, because a prototype that only works on a workstation has not answered anything about the field.
  3. Days 5 to 7, harden and hand over. Run it the way it will be used, fix what breaks, document the decisions and the dead ends, and hand over something the client's own people can run without me in the room.

Why it holds up

Every build on this page was shaped by a live deadline, a stage, a competition, a public demonstration, a camp starting on a fixed date. That is the same pressure a sprint puts on the work, and it rewards the same habits: remove the binding constraint first and keep everything else boring.

The ramp Stated plainly

Not every field moves at the same speed

Saying otherwise would be a claim you could check and find false. A domain that sits next to tech is productive in days, because the vocabulary is already shared and the failure modes are familiar. A domain far from tech takes longer, because the reading comes first and there is no shortcut through it. I scope and price against that curve rather than pretend it is flat.

The ramp is really about where the unknowns sit. In an IoT problem the unknowns are technical, a sensor that drifts or a link that drops, and I already have the instruments to find them. In a field like labour law the unknowns are in the domain itself: what the rules say, which exceptions matter, which answer would be wrong in a way only a specialist would notice. Those cannot be debugged, only read. So the first days of a far-field sprint go to sources and to questions for the client, and the build starts later, but it starts on firmer ground.

Jun 2023 – Apr 2024 RoboAI Hub

The intelligence layer of a humanoid that went on stage

The robot was built by a team led by the scientist Narayan Jangid over three years. I built its software: face recognition, the conversation engine and the speech pipeline, in three months, as a diploma student, with no documentation to work from. I was on stage when it was shown.

The constraint that bound

Everything had to run on a Raspberry Pi 3B+ with 1 GB of RAM, sealed inside a robot body with no screen or keyboard attached. That single fact decided more of the design than any feature did. Face recognition, a language model client, text to speech and motor control all had to share one small board, and a crash would happen in front of an audience.

How I did it

  1. Made the Pi reachable without a screen. SSH through PuTTY, VNC for a remote desktop, and the Wi-Fi written into wpa_supplicant.conf by hand. Every deployment and every debugging session after that ran over the network.
  2. Removed the blocker before writing features. Face recognition depends on DLIB, and it would not install on the Pi. I compiled it from source with CMake on the Pi itself, a four-hour build on 1 GB of RAM, because nothing else could move until it existed.
  3. Gave recognition a memory. The module identifies people in real time, remembers who it has already greeted, and answers team members differently from strangers, so the robot does not greet the same person every few seconds.
  4. Built the voice. A conversation engine streaming GPT-3.5-turbo, with a persona that shifts by speaker, voiced through a gTTS and ffmpeg pipeline tuned for natural cadence.
  5. Closed the loop on the joints. In the second phase I designed a UART protocol between an Arduino Nano and the Pi to read joint positions from potentiometers in real time, and calibrated 25 kg·cm metal-geared servos: PWM tuning, duty-cycle mapping and movement sequencing across shoulder, hand and base.
  6. Bought back the memory. Raspbian Lite with a hand-installed lightweight desktop kept about seventy per cent of the RAM free for the application.

Why it worked

The last step is the one that mattered on the day. On 1 GB, the difference between a full desktop and a minimal one is the difference between a robot that runs and one that stops mid-sentence. Treating memory as the design constraint from the first hour, rather than as a problem to fix at the end, is what let four heavy subsystems share one small board on a public stage eight months later.

Sep 2022 – May 2023 Government Polytechnic College, Jodhpur

A face-recognition attendance system, and the four people who built it

I led a four-person team to build a real-time face-recognition attendance system that runs fully locally, with no AI APIs. It won first prize at the college's annual project competition and was selected as the best project across all computer science final-year teams.

The problem

Attendance was marked by hand in a register. The replacement had to work in an ordinary classroom, on ordinary lab machines, without an internet connection or a paid service behind it, and it had to produce a record a teacher could actually use.

How we did it

  1. Unblocked the environment first. The first problem was not the algorithm. Standard DLIB wheels failed on the lab machines, so I compiled DLIB from source to unblock the pipeline before anyone wrote a line of recognition logic. A team of four cannot work in parallel while the foundation will not install.
  2. Kept recognition on the machine. Detection and recognition with DLIB and OpenCV against a database of facial encodings for known students. Nothing leaves the room, which is also why it works without a network.
  3. Decoupled the camera. The video is streamed over Wi-Fi through Iriun Webcam, so the system does not depend on whatever camera happens to be plugged into the room's computer.
  4. Built for the person using it. A Tkinter desktop interface that runs offline, and automated Excel session reports with name, timestamp and date, so the output is a file a teacher already knows how to open.

What it shows

Each decision removes a dependency: on a wheel that would not install, on a cloud API, on a specific camera, on a network. A system with fewer things it depends on has fewer ways to fail on the day it is judged.

  • Result 1st prize, best final-year project
June 2025 MBM University

KineLink, a gesture-controlled arm in seven days

Move your arm and the robot mirrors it in real time over serial, with no cloud. I studied the DAWN remote-pilot system behind Japan's Avatar Robot Café, worked out how its piloting loop held together, and built my own version in seven days. A laptop, a camera, connections soldered on zero-PCB, and a base made from an empty tape roller. No lab and no equipment budget.

The research step

This is the sprint method at small scale. The first part of the week went to understanding someone else's system well enough to rebuild its architecture, not its parts: a person's movement is captured, turned into joint targets, and sent to a machine that follows. Once the loop is clear, every component can be the cheapest one that closes it.

How I did it

  1. Pose from one camera. MediaPipe Pose tracks shoulder, elbow and wrist landmarks from a single camera feed.
  2. Geometry, not a model, for the angles. I compute shoulder vertical, shoulder horizontal and elbow flex with arctan2 on the landmark positions and map each to a servo position. It is exact, fast and needs no training data.
  3. A hand as a switch. MediaPipe Hands detects a closed fist and drives the gripper, so grabbing is the same gesture for the person and the robot.
  4. A protocol small enough to parse anywhere. Commands stream as index, angle, terminator, parsed by an Arduino or an ESP32. The arm is hardware-agnostic by design, so the controller can change without touching the vision side.
  5. Two modes, one toggle. A Tkinter interface with per-joint sliders for manual calibration, and an AI mode with full real-time body tracking. Calibrating by hand first is what makes the tracked mode trustworthy.

November 2024 RoboAI Hub

Servo control across two planes

An arm that moves through the XY and XZ planes with interpolation I wrote myself, driven wirelessly from a laptop over Bluetooth, and reliable enough to run in front of the public three times.

The platform decision

The build started on an Arduino Mega and hit that board's wireless ceiling. The choice was to work around the limit or change the platform. I migrated the entire codebase to an ESP32 and kept the build moving. Changing boards mid-project is expensive, but living with a ceiling you already know about is more expensive, because every later feature pays for it.

How I did it

  1. Wrote the maths the library did not have. No motion library did what was needed, so the angle-to-duty-cycle conversion is calculated by hand from the servo's pulse range, with PWM at 50 Hz.
  2. Made motion smooth. Step-based interpolation, so the arm moves through intermediate positions instead of jumping between targets. Jerky motion is not only ugly, it loads the joints and shakes the payload.
  3. Coordinated the joints. Several servos driven together so a move across XY or XZ is one motion rather than a sequence of single-joint moves.
  4. Tested where it would be used. Wireless control from a laptop over Bluetooth, demonstrated live rather than on a bench.

Where it ran

PROMETEO 2025 at IIT Jodhpur, where a professor recommended advancing the work; Taabar Mela in Jodhpur, in operation before an open public audience; and an MBM University faculty workshop on intelligent embedded systems. The arm on the workbench in this site solves the same problem the other way round: instead of joint angles in, a target point in space, and the angles worked out with two-link inverse kinematics.

13–18 January 2025 PM SHRI Kendriya Vidyalaya, Mount Abu

Five days of AI and machine learning, written from nothing

A curriculum built from scratch under the RoboAI Hub initiative: Python fundamentals, core AI, computer vision and the mathematics underneath the models. Sixty-eight students took part across the five days. I co-trained it with a colleague, Neha.

What made it hard

There was no existing course to adapt, five days is short, and school students arrive with very different starting points. Teaching AI as a list of tools would have left them able to repeat names and unable to explain anything.

How it was built

The subjects were chosen so that each one gives the next something to stand on: Python first, so the rest can be done rather than watched; core AI ideas next; computer vision as the part students can see working on a camera; and the mathematics underneath the models, so what they saw has an explanation. It is the same instinct as the research sprints, applied to people instead of a prototype: find what the rest depends on and put it first.
